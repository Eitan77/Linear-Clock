Total Time Spent: >28 hours, with a LOT more to come once I can assemble, code, test, and revise. The optimization of the design and the UI is what I think will take the most time on this project. 

Dates: I started this project on the first of June, and I hope to assemble it as soon as I can get the parts! 

Time Spent Per Day: I have spent around three to four hours per day, on as many days of the week as I can manage. 

6/25, 4 Hours - Created concept design for clock shell. I started by making sketches of what I wanted the clock to look like and doing research on examples of linear progress bars that have been made before, mainly on things such as audio mixers and old radio hardware. I decided that I wanted to do a 60-60-24 layout because I was debating doing a simplified 30-30-12 layout, but it really ruined the aesthetic, and I think it got rid of the purpose of the piece. I made a couple of sketches in Onshape, and then started looking into what size the LEDS should be, and ended up with an inch in height, so it would be visible from a distance but not too large in the enclosure. 

6/26, 3 Hours - I started to model the enclosure in Onshape. After deciding on the width of the LEDS, I made an outline of the enclosure and realized it would be over 20 inches long, so I re-worked the width of the LEDs so that it would all fit into 18 inches. That way, it could be 3D printed in one piece on a larger printer. I wanted it to be printed in one piece for aesthetic reasons, so the outside could be as smooth as possible. I added a recess for the acrylic panel to sit in so that it would not fall out of the front, and rather than going for fastners or some other type of hardware that might be visible in the final product, I decided to make it kinda like a sandwich, and then have it all sit in together and be held against the recess with screws from the back. I modeled the diffuser guide after making basic models of the LEDs for reference, and then created an assembly of all the parts to test it out. 

6/27, 5 Hours - I continued with the model and looked into electronics. For the model, I worked on creating the fastening system, which works by implementing small screw wedges that slide into the back of the enclosure and are retained via friction. I did this for 2 reasons: One, it allows me to slide in all of the interior components without having to manipulate them around screws, and two, it makes changing out any components a very simple process. After creating the fastening system, I began to research the different types of electronics I could use, and settled on a custom PCB because the array of the LEDS I am going with is very unique. For the brains of the clock, after some research, I settled on the Arduino Nano because it has good LED support, is small, so it would fit in the case, and is  inexpensive compared to the other Arduino models. For the LEDS, I went with the WS2812-V5 LEDs because of their low power draw and easy daisy chain control method. They also do not need a dedicated resistor each, which is really helpful. I researched how they would interact with the Nano and then looked into the other components, such as switches and the usbc breakout.

6/28, 4 Hours - I finished enclosure design and began working on PCB. I re-sized the diffuser and added a couple of finishing touches on the enclosure, mainly the final outer size and the dimensions of some of the sketches. I then started work on the PCB. I needed to find a program to design the PCB, and so I started researching different platforms and eventually came across EasyEDA. I looked at some YouTube tutorials, and it looked pretty easy, so I signed up for it and spent some time checking it out and getting myself familiar with the tools. I looked at some simple tutorials on how to route traces and use components, and after a few hours, felt confident enough to use the program for the project. 

6/29, 4 Hours - I continued with the PCB design. I brought in the LED models and the Nano, as well as the control buttons, and started researching how they work together. I looked at a bunch of different wiring diagrams and read some online forums for the LEDs. I then got to work connecting them in EasyEDA, and then converted the schematic to a PCB, and spent a bunch of time making sure the LEDS were all spaced out properly, because they need to be exact for an even LED distribution onto the clock face. I watched some tutorials for how to use different tools in EasyEDA, and then was able to get them spaced out as I wanted them to, and to get the overall shape of the board right. I routed all of the traces on the PCB and moved the components around to different layers so that they would be in the proper places for the final assembly.

6/30, 4 Hours - Finished PCB design, refining enclosure design. Today I finished the PCB design by going back and adding the mounting holes, which took a lot longer than expected because I found the dimension tools very hard to work with. I experimented with different methods of connecting wires from the power and the switches to the PCB, and ultimately settled on a through-hole design, where it would be a lot easier for me to solder the final board together. I adjusted the spacing on some of the LEDS, and with that, version 1 of the PCB was completed. I then went back to the enclosure after having an idea of how to improve the screw wedges. I changed the design of the slots, making them easier to insert and remove, and then tested it with an assembly to see how it would all fit together. I changed them to hold nuts that could be dropped in and glued, but maybe for later versions, I will change them to use heat-set inserts for stability. 

7/1, 4 Hours - I am working on a PCB mounting bracket. I had an idea that I could make the PCB smaller and then have it held in place with a 3D printed bracket to save on production costs. I went back into Onshape and started modeling the bracket around a STEP file export of the PCB I got from EasyEDA. I designed the bracket to have a slight recess in the front so that the PCB could sit inside, and then it would fasten through to the back with a set of M4 screws. This way, it could be held in the proper position, without having to be simply larger to hold its position, saving a ton on PCB fabrication costs. I then added the bracket to the main design and made sure that it all fit before going back and slightly skeletonizing the PCB mounting bracket to save on filament use and print time.
